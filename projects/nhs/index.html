University of York · Data Science coursework · May 2025

Sometimes the answer is "the data can't tell you."

I turned six raw NHS datasets into a clean relational database, then tested what drives cancelled appointments. The honest finding was that none of the recorded factors predict it, and the useful result came from somewhere else.

The data

The coursework gave six raw CSV files covering appointments, billing, insurance, prescriptions, surgery and test records. Between them they describe 556,692 appointments for 400,000 patients across 252 hospitals, 3,143 departments and 27,752 healthcare professionals.

Building the database

  • Designed an entity-relationship diagram in Chen notation to map how patients, appointments, professionals, departments and hospitals relate.
  • Normalised the data to third normal form (3NF) and loaded it into SQLite, so every fact is stored once and joins are reliable.
  • Wrote SQL reports with outer joins, for example hospitals per city, departments per hospital and preferred patients.

Fixing the question first

The original notebook filtered on a No-Show status, but the data doesn't have one: appointments are only Completed, Cancelled or Scheduled. That filter silently returned nothing. I fixed it and used cancellation as the closest available measure, giving 500,830 completed or cancelled appointments with a 10.0% cancellation rate.

Testing for drivers

MethodWhat I testedResult
Chi-square + Cramér's VAge, gender, weekday, hour, department and hospital against cancellationNo association: all p > 0.2, V < 0.03
Logistic regression (class-weighted)Predicting cancellation from those attributesROC-AUC 0.50, no better than chance
K-means clusteringGrouping patients by how they use services (k = 3, silhouette 0.66)A small, very heavy-use group

An AUC of 0.50 means the model is guessing. Rather than tune it until a number looked impressive, I reported that these attributes carry no signal and named the data that would: how far ahead the appointment was booked, and each patient's past attendance.

The useful finding

Clustering found that 2% of patients (6,337 people) account for about 15% of all appointments. For planning, that small group matters far more than demographics: it's where follow-up, reminders or care coordination would have the biggest effect.

Ethics

Health data is sensitive, so the project includes an ethics analysis alongside the technical work. A model with no predictive power is also a reminder of why results like this should never drive decisions about individual patients.

What I learned

Check the data before the model.

The biggest bug was a status that didn't exist. Looking at the actual values first would have caught it immediately.

A negative result is still a result.

"These fields can't predict cancellations" is a real, useful answer, as long as you say what data would.

Good schema design pays off.

Once the data was normalised, every later query and analysis was simpler and more trustworthy.
